# Foreword: A Million Dollars Is Just Arithmetic Until It Isn't

Let me begin with the sentence most books in this genre bury on page two hundred: a one-person business earning $1 million in a year is possible, and it is also rare. Most solo businesses earn far less than that. Many earn less than a part-time job, and a large share never earn anything at all. Nothing in the pages that follow changes those facts, and nothing here is a promise about what you will make. What this book offers instead is a method for finding out, quickly and cheaply, whether your particular idea has a path to seven figures, and for building that path with as few wasted months as possible.

The reason a book like this can exist now is that the cost of certain kinds of work has collapsed. Drafting a market research summary used to take a week of reading. Producing a first version of a landing page, a spreadsheet model, a sales script, a support knowledge base or a small piece of software used to require hiring someone or spending months learning a craft. Today a capable person with a good language model, a few no-code tools and clear judgment can do the first draft of nearly all of it in an afternoon. That does not make the business easy. It moves the difficulty. The scarce things are no longer typing speed or technical access. They are taste, trust, distribution, and the discipline to point all of this cheap capacity at a problem someone will actually pay to solve.

I have organised the book as a sprint, because speed is the real subject. Speed does not mean cutting corners or skipping validation. In practice the fastest route to a durable business is the one that kills bad ideas early, because every month spent on a flawed offer is a month you cannot spend on a sound one. The first part of the book is about arithmetic and selection: what a million dollars of annual revenue actually requires in price and customers, what one human being can operate, which markets deserve your attention, and how to design and test an offer before you build it. The middle part is about leverage: where artificial intelligence genuinely multiplies your output in research, building, marketing, delivery and support, and where it quietly makes things worse. The final part is about durability: systems, contractors, retention, taxes and legal basics, burnout, and the several different shapes a path to $1 million can take.

You will notice that I use hypothetical numbers throughout. Every dollar figure in this book is an illustration unless it is a widely known public fact, and I will say so when it is. I do this deliberately. A made-up success story with a precise bank balance teaches you nothing you can verify, and it quietly teaches you to expect an outcome that most people will not reach. A transparent calculation, by contrast, lets you change the inputs and see what your own situation would require. When I write that a business sells 1,000 licences at $1,000, I am not describing a real company. I am handing you a model you can break, adjust and rebuild with your own assumptions.

Two further notes on honesty. First, anything involving tax, law, insurance, employment status or platform rules varies by country, by state and by year, and I am not your accountant or your lawyer. When a chapter touches those subjects, it will describe the shape of the questions to ask and the traps to avoid, and then it will tell you to confirm the details with a qualified professional where you live. Second, artificial intelligence tools change fast. I will name categories of tools and a few widely used products as examples, but pricing, features and terms shift constantly, so treat every tool mention as a starting point for your own check rather than an endorsement carved in stone.

What kind of reader is this book for? It is for the person who already has some skill, domain knowledge or access that others value, and who wants to turn it into a product or service they can run mostly alone. It is for the consultant who is tired of selling hours, the employee with expertise and a quiet side project, the creator with an audience but no real offer, and the technical person who can build things but has never had to sell them. It is not for someone looking for a secret trick, a guaranteed system, or a way to make money without making anything people want. If you finish a chapter and feel that it asked you to do uncomfortable, specific work such as calling strangers, raising a price, or deleting a feature you love, then the book is doing its job.

Each chapter follows a similar shape. It opens with a concrete problem, explains the idea from first principles, shows a worked example with the arithmetic in the open, names the common mistakes and how to spot them in your own work, and ends with an exercise you can complete in a week. I would encourage you to actually do the exercises. A reader who completes twenty small exercises over twenty weeks will know far more about their market than a reader who devours the whole book in a weekend and changes nothing. The exercises are small on purpose, and together they form the sprint.

A word about the phrase one-person business. It rarely means a person who never speaks to anyone. Successful solo operators lean on customers, on contractors for specific tasks, on software for repetitive ones, and on a handful of trusted peers for advice. What the phrase really describes is a company where the owner remains the only full-time employee, carries the whole decision load, and designs the work so that adding revenue does not require adding payroll. That is a design constraint, and like any constraint it forces creativity. It also forces trade-offs, some of which you may not like. A solo business that grows past what one person can safely carry will, sooner or later, have to become something else, and we will talk about how to recognise that moment instead of pretending it will not arrive.

Finally, a thought about why anyone would want this path in the first place. The appeal is not only money. It is the control over your time, the clarity of knowing exactly where your income comes from, and the satisfaction of building something small enough to understand completely. Those are real benefits, and they are worth weighing honestly against the risks: irregular income, isolation, the absence of a safety net, and the temptation to work every waking hour because nobody else will. If after reading you decide that a conventional job suits you better, that is a perfectly good outcome, and it will have cost you far less than discovering it eighteen months and a lot of savings later.

Turn the page, and let us start where every sound plan starts: with the math of what a million dollars actually means, and with an honest look at how many things have to go right for it to happen.

How should you read this book? My suggestion is to read one chapter at a time and then stop. Do the exercise at the end before you open the next chapter, even when the exercise feels too small to matter. The chapters build on each other in a deliberate order: market before offer, offer before price, price before unit economics, unit economics before automation. Reversing that order is the most common way solo founders waste a year. They automate a process nobody wanted, or they build a beautiful product for a market too small to pay for it, because they started with the exciting tools instead of the dull question of who would write a cheque and why.

You should also know what I assume you already have. I assume you can write clearly enough to explain an idea to a stranger, that you can tolerate a little technical fiddling, and that you have some savings or income that lets you run experiments without risking your housing or your health. If you do not have that cushion, the advice is to build slowly alongside a job, shrink the experiments, and treat the first year as an education that happens to pay a little. There is no shame in that route, and it is statistically the saner one. Founders who quit everything for a single large bet make better stories and worse expected outcomes than founders who test ten small things while employed.

One more promise, and then we begin. I will not tell you that artificial intelligence makes competition disappear. It does the opposite. Every tool that makes you faster makes every other solo operator faster too, which means that the basic ability to produce content, code and copy cheaply is no longer an advantage. It is the price of entry. The durable advantages are the ones a model cannot generate for you: a specific audience that trusts you, proprietary knowledge or data gathered by doing real work, relationships, a reputation for reliability, and judgment about what to leave out. This book spends much of its length on building those, with the tools as accelerants rather than substitutes. Keep that distinction in mind and you will avoid the most expensive mistake of the current moment, which is confusing output with value.
